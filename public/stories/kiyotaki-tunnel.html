Kiyotaki Tunnel: Kyoto's Cursed Tunnel of the Dead

9 min read SSSSS Coordinates: 35.0094 N, 135.6486 E
🛣

Japan has a particular genre of haunted place: the cursed tunnel. There are many, but few are as feared as the Kiyotaki Tunnel, a narrow, single-lane passage cut through a mountain near Kyoto. On the surface it is simply an old, cramped road tunnel, controlled by a traffic signal because it is too tight for cars to pass in both directions. But in Japanese ghost lore, Kiyotaki is something else entirely: a gateway heavy with death and bad luck, wrapped in some of the most specific and unsettling urban legends in the country.

What makes Kiyotaki frightening is not a single dramatic ghost but a web of small, precise rules and warnings, the kind of legends that make drivers grip the wheel a little tighter as they wait for the light to change.

A Tunnel Built in Hardship

The Kiyotaki Tunnel was originally constructed as a railway tunnel in the early 20th century before later being adapted for road use. Like many infrastructure projects of its era, its construction is remembered as grueling and dangerous, and local lore holds that the work came at a human cost. That association of the tunnel with hardship and death during its building is the root from which its darker reputation grew.

Over the decades, the tunnel accumulated a reputation as a cursed spot, a place connected to misfortune and tragedy, and the legends multiplied until Kiyotaki became a staple of Japanese ghost stories and a pilgrimage site for those who go looking for a scare.

The Legends

Kiyotaki's legends are famous for their specificity. One of the best known concerns the traffic signal at the entrance: it is said that if the light turns blue or green as you approach, you should not enter, because something unnatural is luring you in, and misfortune or death may follow. A red light, by this logic, is the safe one. It inverts the ordinary rules of the road into something sinister.

Another legend holds that the tunnel's length appears to change, that it feels longer than it should, as if it does not want to let you out. And perhaps the most chilling: that if you glance at your side mirror while inside the tunnel, you may catch sight of a figure, often described as a woman, in the reflection, someone who is not actually in your car. Drivers are warned not to look.

If the signal turns green as you approach, the legend says, do not enter. Red is the safe light at Kiyotaki. And whatever you do, do not look in your mirror inside the tunnel.

The Woman and the Dark

Beyond the rules, Kiyotaki is tied to sightings of apparitions, most famously a ghostly woman said to appear near or within the tunnel. Some accounts describe her reflected in mirrors or glimpsed at the edge of the headlights; others place her at the mouth of the tunnel, a lingering presence connected to the site's sorrowful history. Phantom sounds and an overwhelming sense of dread are also commonly reported by those who pass through, especially at night.

The tunnel's physical qualities amplify everything. It is genuinely narrow, dim, and old, the kind of place that feels oppressive even without a ghost story attached. Add the weight of a century of legend, and the simple act of waiting at a red light before entering becomes an exercise in nerve.

Driving Kiyotaki

The Kiyotaki Tunnel is still an active road tunnel, and it can be driven through today. A traffic signal controls the single lane, forcing drivers to wait their turn, a wait that, given the legends, feels longer than it is. For fans of Japanese ghost lore, passing through Kiyotaki is a rite of passage, best attempted after dark, with the stories fresh in mind and a careful eye kept away from the mirror.

Whether Kiyotaki is genuinely cursed or simply the perfect stage for fear, an old, tight, isolated tunnel wrapped in decades of dread, it remains one of Japan's most famous haunted places. It proves that a haunted site does not need a castle or a crumbling mansion. Sometimes all it takes is a dark hole through a mountain, a flickering signal, and a warning never to look at your own reflection.
