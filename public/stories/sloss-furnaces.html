Sloss Furnaces: The Haunted Ironworks and Slag the Foreman

11 min read SSSS Coordinates: 33.5207 N, 86.7690 W
🔥

Most haunted places are quiet, grand, or decayed: castles, hotels, graveyards. Sloss Furnaces is none of those. It is a monster of rust and iron, a sprawling industrial skeleton of pipes, towers, and blast furnaces looming over Birmingham, Alabama. For nearly ninety years it turned raw ore into pig iron and, too often, turned men into casualties. Today it stands preserved as a museum, and it has a second reputation as one of the most haunted industrial sites in America, presided over, legend says, by the ghost of a foreman so cruel that even hell seemed to spit him back out.

Sloss ran from 1882 to 1971, at the heart of Birmingham's iron and steel boom. The work was punishing and lethal: extreme heat, molten metal, toxic gas, and heavy machinery, all handled by laborers, many of them Black workers and convicts leased out under brutal conditions, in an era with little regard for safety. Deaths were common. That grim human toll is the real foundation of everything that came after.

The Legend of Slag Wormwood

The story that made Sloss famous centers on a foreman named James "Slag" Wormwood, said to have overseen the graveyard shift in the early 1900s. As the legend goes, Wormwood was tyrannical, driving his men to take deadly shortcuts to boost production and win favor with the bosses. Dozens of workers are said to have died under him during his years on the killing shift, and he showed them no mercy and no mourning.

Then, the tale says, Wormwood himself lost his footing on top of the highest furnace, nicknamed Big Alice, and fell into the molten iron, dying instantly in the same fire he had forced so many others to face. Historians point out that no record of a foreman by that name has been confirmed, and much of the story is likely folklore built up around the site's genuinely deadly history. But folklore or not, the legend gave Sloss a villain, and the reports that followed gave him a presence.

"Get Back to Work"

The reports from Sloss have a distinct, industrial flavor. Where other hauntings offer sad ladies and crying children, Sloss offers an angry boss. Visitors and night watchmen over the decades have described being shoved hard from behind on the catwalks and stairs, hearing a gruff voice bark orders like "get back to work," and, most strikingly, being burned, feeling sudden searing heat or finding unexplained burn-like marks on their skin after an encounter.

One widely repeated account tells of a night watchman in the 1920s who was attacked by a badly burned figure that tried to push him up the stairs, shouting at him to get back to work. Whether or not any single story holds up, the pattern, an aggressive male presence tied to the furnaces and the theme of forced labor, runs consistently through Sloss's reputation.

People do not just see things at Sloss. They say they get burned, shoved on the catwalks, and shouted at by a voice that only knows one command: get back to work.

The Real Horror Beneath the Legend

It is worth separating the ghost story from the history, because the history is genuinely dark on its own. Sloss operated in an era of convict leasing, when incarcerated men, disproportionately Black, were rented out as industrial labor and worked in lethal conditions with little recourse. The heat, the molten iron, and the toxic environment killed and maimed workers regularly. The "haunting" of Sloss, in a real sense, is the memory of that suffering, dramatized into a single villain and a set of ghost stories.

That may be why the legend has such staying power. Slag Wormwood is fiction, but the deaths were real, and the anger and pain that visitors say they feel at Sloss have a true source in the ground beneath the rust.

Visiting Sloss

Today Sloss Furnaces is a National Historic Landmark and museum, preserved as one of the best surviving examples of American industrial history. By day, visitors tour the furnaces, sheds, and towering iron structures. By night, it leans hard into its reputation, hosting some of the most popular paranormal tours and Halloween events in the South, sending people into the dark maze of pipes to see whether the foreman is still on shift.

Sloss is a different kind of haunted place, not a mansion or a crypt but a machine, a vast rusting engine that consumed human lives for the better part of a century. Whether or not Slag Wormwood ever existed, the furnaces remember what happened in their heat, and at Sloss, the dead do not rest quietly. They go back to work.

Frequently Asked

Why is Sloss Furnaces haunted?
It operated from 1882 to 1971 as one of the deadliest workplaces of its era, killing laborers with heat, molten iron, and machinery. The famous legend concerns a cruel foreman, "Slag" Wormwood, said to have driven men to their deaths before falling into a furnace himself. Visitors report burns, shoves, and an angry presence.
Who was Slag Wormwood?
The legendary foreman at the heart of the haunting, said to have pushed workers into deadly risks on the night shift before falling into a furnace and dying in the molten iron. Historians note no record confirms him and the tale is largely folklore, but his angry spirit is blamed for attacks on visitors.
What do people experience at Sloss?
Being shoved or burned by an unseen presence, voices shouting orders like "get back to work," apparitions of workers, footsteps on the catwalks, and cold spots. The site has been investigated by many paranormal teams and featured on television.
