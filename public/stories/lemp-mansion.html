Lemp Mansion: The Beer Dynasty That Destroyed Itself

13 min read ☠☠☠☠☆ Coordinates: 38.5942° N, 90.2242° W
🍺

In the late 1800s, the Lemp family was one of the wealthiest and most powerful in St. Louis. They had introduced lager beer to America, built a brewing empire that rivaled Anheuser-Busch, and lived in a mansion that reflected their fortune. By the middle of the 20th century, the family was effectively extinct — its empire gone, its fortune scattered, and four of its members dead by their own hands. The Lemp story is not really a ghost story. It is a story about a family that destroyed itself, and a house that seems to have kept them.

The Empire

Johann "Adam" Lemp founded the brewery, but it was his son William Lemp Sr. who built it into a giant. The Lemp Brewery was one of the first to introduce true German lager to America, and it made the family enormously rich. They built their mansion near the brewery, connected to it — and to a network of natural limestone caves beneath the city — by underground passages. The caves were cool year-round, ideal for lagering beer before refrigeration, and the Lemps used them not just for brewing but for leisure, reportedly building an underground auditorium and even a swimming pool in the caverns beneath their empire.

For a time, they had everything. And then the deaths began.

Four Suicides

The unraveling of the Lemp family is one of the most concentrated series of tragedies in American business history.

Frederick Lemp (1901): The first blow was not a suicide but a loss. Frederick, William Sr.'s favorite son and the heir he had groomed to run the empire, died suddenly at 28 from heart failure. William Sr. never recovered from the grief.

William Lemp Sr. (1904): Three years after Frederick's death — and shortly after the death of his close friend, another brewing magnate — William Sr. shot himself in the head in his bedroom in the mansion. He was the first Lemp to die in the house by his own hand. He would not be the last.

Elsa Lemp (1920): William Sr.'s daughter Elsa, the wealthiest heiress in St. Louis, shot herself in her own home during a troubled marriage. When her brother William Jr. arrived and was told she had died, he reportedly said only, "That's the Lemp family for you."

William Lemp Jr. (1922): Prohibition destroyed the brewery. The Lemp empire, built on beer, could not survive a nation that had outlawed it, and the business was sold off for a fraction of its worth. In 1922, William Jr. shot himself in the mansion — in the same house where his father had died 18 years earlier.

Charles Lemp (1949): The last act. Charles Lemp, aging and in poor health, continued to live in the mansion long after the family's glory had ended. In 1949, he shot his dog and then shot himself in the mansion — the third Lemp to die by suicide inside those walls. He left a note: "In case I am found dead, blame it on no one but me."

The Monkey Face Boy

Beyond the documented suicides, Lemp Mansion carries a darker and less verifiable legend — the story of a hidden child. According to the account, there was an illegitimate Lemp son, born with a physical or developmental condition, who was kept hidden away in the attic of the mansion, out of sight of society, for his entire life. He is known in the mansion's lore as the "Monkey Face Boy."

Historians debate whether this child existed — the documentation is thin, and the story may have grown in the retelling. But it is one of the most frequently reported hauntings in the mansion. Visitors and staff describe a child's presence in the attic: the feeling of being watched, a small face at the window seen from outside, and the sense of a lonely child still confined to the space where, if the story is true, he spent a hidden and joyless life.

The House Today

Lemp Mansion did not stay empty. After the family's collapse, it eventually became a restaurant and inn — and one of the most actively haunted commercial locations in America. Staff report glasses that fly off the bar, doors that lock and unlock on their own, phantom footsteps on the stairs, and apparitions of the Lemp family members who died there. The piano reportedly plays on its own. Guests who stay overnight report the covers being pulled, knocking, and the sense of not being alone.

Life Magazine once named Lemp Mansion one of the most haunted houses in America, and it has embraced the title. It hosts ghost tours, murder mystery dinners, and Halloween events. People come to eat dinner in the rooms where a family shot itself apart across half a century — and, often, to feel that the family never quite left.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Lemp Mansion haunted?
Lemp Mansion in St. Louis is considered one of the most haunted houses in America. The Lemp family suffered four suicides — three inside the mansion. Visitors and staff at the mansion, now a restaurant and inn, report apparitions, phantom footsteps, doors locking on their own, and the presence of a hidden child known as the Monkey Face Boy.
How many Lemp family members died by suicide?
Four. William Lemp Sr. (1904), his daughter Elsa (1920), his son William Jr. (1922), and his son Charles (1949). Three of the four died inside Lemp Mansion. The collapse followed the death of favorite son Frederick and the loss of their brewing empire during Prohibition.
Who is the Monkey Face Boy at Lemp Mansion?
The "Monkey Face Boy" is a child said to have been hidden in the mansion's attic — reportedly an illegitimate family child with a physical condition, kept out of sight. Visitors and staff report a child's presence in the attic. Historical documentation is debated, but it's one of the mansion's most reported hauntings.
