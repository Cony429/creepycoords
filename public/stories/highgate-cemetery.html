Highgate Cemetery: London's Vampire and the Victorian Dead

12 min read SSSS Coordinates: 51.5670 N, 0.1470 W
🎋

Some cemeteries are simply where the dead are kept. Highgate is something else. Climbing up the hillside in north London, choked with ivy and leaning headstones, its Victorian tombs collapsing into one another beneath a canopy of overgrown trees, it looks less like a graveyard and more like a lost city being slowly reclaimed by the earth. For over a century it has been London's most atmospheric burial ground, and in the early 1970s it became the stage for one of the strangest episodes in modern British folklore: a genuine vampire panic.

Opened in 1839, Highgate was one of the "Magnificent Seven," a ring of grand private cemeteries built around London to relieve the overcrowded, disease-ridden churchyards of the inner city. The Victorians did not hide from death. They built for it, with soaring Gothic architecture, symbolic statuary, and grand avenues of tombs. Highgate's West Cemetery, with its Egyptian Avenue and the sunken Circle of Lebanon, is the finest and eeriest example of that vision anywhere in Britain.

The Magnificent Decay

By the mid-20th century, Highgate had fallen into ruin. The company that owned it went bankrupt, and the West Cemetery was left to rot. Nature moved in fast. Brambles swallowed graves, tree roots cracked open tombs, and vandals broke into vaults. It was in this abandoned, overgrown state, a place of genuine dread after dark, that the legend took hold.

The Highgate Vampire

In the late 1960s, reports began to circulate of a tall, dark figure seen inside the cemetery gates at dusk. Witnesses described a towering shape with a deathly face and eyes that seemed to glow red. Some said animals were found dead nearby, drained of blood. In an era hungry for the occult, the story caught fire.

Two local men, each claiming expertise in the supernatural, appointed themselves the cemetery's vampire hunters, and their rivalry poured fuel on the panic. One of them appeared on television in early 1970, announcing his intention to hunt and destroy the entity. The broadcast did exactly what you would expect.

On the night of 13 March 1970, a mob poured over the cemetery walls after dark, armed with stakes, crosses, garlic, and holy water, hunting a vampire among the graves of Victorian London.

The Night of the Hunt

The vampire hunt of March 1970 is one of the strangest crowd events in modern London history. Despite police attempts to keep people out, dozens of amateur hunters flooded into the cemetery, convinced they were about to confront the undead. In the months and years that followed, the panic curdled into something uglier. Tombs were broken open. Coffins were dragged out. At least one body was reportedly desecrated, staked, or worse, in the belief that it was a vampire.

Whatever people thought they saw at Highgate, the damage done to the cemetery in the name of hunting it was terribly real. The episode became a cautionary tale about how quickly suggestion, media, and fear can turn a crowd loose on a graveyard.

What Walks Among the Graves

Strip away the vampire hysteria and Highgate still has a deep catalogue of ghost stories. Long before the 1970s, visitors reported a tall dark figure that seemed to hover among the tombs, gazing out, before vanishing. Others told of a spectral cyclist, a gaunt old woman rushing between the graves searching for children she was said to have murdered, and a face that appeared and dissolved in the gloom. The overgrown paths of the West Cemetery have an undeniable atmosphere, the kind of place where the mind readily supplies a watcher behind every leaning stone.

Today Highgate has been rescued. A charity of Friends of Highgate Cemetery has cleared the worst of the decay, stabilized the tombs, and reopened the grounds to visitors, who can walk the Egyptian Avenue and the Circle of Lebanon by guided tour. It is beautiful now, in a solemn, Gothic way. But go at closing time, when the light drains out of the trees and the tombs turn to silhouettes, and it is not hard to understand how a whole city once convinced itself that something was moving in the dark.

Frequently Asked

What is the Highgate Vampire?
The focus of a widely reported paranormal panic in London in the early 1970s. Witnesses claimed to see a tall, dark figure with glowing eyes inside the cemetery. Two self-styled vampire hunters publicized rival investigations, and on 13 March 1970 a crowd climbed the walls hoping to confront the creature.
Is Highgate Cemetery haunted?
It has a long reputation for the uncanny. Beyond the vampire panic, visitors have reported a tall dark figure among the graves, a spectral cyclist, a mad old woman searching for murdered children, and cold, watchful sensations along the older overgrown paths.
Who is buried in Highgate Cemetery?
Its most visited grave is that of Karl Marx, marked by a large bust in the East Cemetery. Other notable burials include author Douglas Adams and scientist Michael Faraday. The cemetery opened in 1839 as one of London's "Magnificent Seven."
Can you visit Highgate Cemetery?
Yes. It is a working cemetery and registered charity in north London. Coordinates: 51.5670 N, 0.1470 W. The atmospheric West Cemetery, with the Egyptian Avenue and Circle of Lebanon, is accessed by guided tour. Tickets are required and Halloween tours sell out quickly.
