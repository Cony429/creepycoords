Greyfriars Kirkyard: The Mackenzie Poltergeist of Edinburgh

11 min read SSSSS Coordinates: 55.9469 N, 3.1911 W
⚰

Greyfriars Kirkyard in the heart of Edinburgh is beautiful in the way old Scottish graveyards are: weathered stones, carved skulls, ivy, and the long shadow of the castle on the hill. It is also, by a large margin, one of the most alarming places in Britain. Since 1999, something in this kirkyard has reportedly attacked hundreds of people, scratching them, bruising them, knocking them unconscious, in one specific corner known as the Covenanters' Prison. Believers call it the Mackenzie Poltergeist, and it is considered one of the best-documented poltergeist cases in the world, simply because of the sheer number of people who say it has hurt them.

To understand the haunting, you have to understand the cruelty that preceded it, because this is a story where the villain of history and the alleged source of the haunting are one and the same man.

Bloody Mackenzie and the Covenanters

In 17th-century Scotland, the Covenanters were Presbyterians who refused to accept the king's authority over their church. They were persecuted brutally, and the man most associated with that persecution was Sir George Mackenzie, the Lord Advocate, remembered to history as "Bloody Mackenzie." In 1679, after a battle, around 1,200 captured Covenanters were brought to a field at the edge of Greyfriars and held there, outdoors, through the Scottish winter, in a makeshift prison with little food or shelter. Many died of exposure, starvation, and execution.

That field is the Covenanters' Prison. And in a dark irony, when Bloody Mackenzie himself died, he was entombed in a grand mausoleum at Greyfriars, only a short distance from the very ground where the people he tormented had suffered and died. For centuries, the two coexisted in uneasy silence. Then, in 1999, the silence broke.

The Poltergeist Awakens

The modern haunting is said to have begun when a homeless man broke into the Mackenzie mausoleum seeking shelter and disturbed the tomb, some versions say he fell through into the vault below, among the remains. Almost immediately afterward, visitors to the adjoining Covenanters' Prison began reporting violent experiences. The timing, a disturbed tomb followed by a sudden wave of attacks, is the heart of the legend.

What people report is unusually physical for a haunting. Not just cold spots and shadows, but injuries: deep scratches appearing on skin, bruises forming after a visit, a sudden crushing pressure, nosebleeds, nausea, and people fainting dead away without warning. Hundreds of such reports have been logged by the tour companies that run the only legal access to the Prison, and dozens of visitors have reportedly required attention after collapsing there.

People do not just feel uneasy in the Covenanters' Prison. They come out with scratches and bruises they did not have going in, and some are carried out after collapsing. Hundreds of such reports have been recorded.

The Locked Prison

Because of the number and intensity of the incidents, the Covenanters' Prison is kept locked. You cannot simply wander into it. The only way in is on an official guided tour, and even then, visitors are often warned about what has happened to others before them. This makes Greyfriars unusual: a haunting with a gatekeeper, a specific locked location that the authorities and tour operators treat with genuine caution.

Skeptics point out that the power of suggestion is strong, that a dark, cold, enclosed space and a frightening story can produce real physical symptoms through fear alone, fainting, racing hearts, even marks from stumbling in the dark. Believers counter that the consistency and the physical injuries set Greyfriars apart from ordinary suggestion. Either way, the documented volume of reports is remarkable.

Visiting Greyfriars

The main kirkyard is open and free, a lovely and historic place to walk, also known as the resting place that inspired some of the names in the Harry Potter books, written nearby. But the Covenanters' Prison at the back is another matter. To enter it you must join a guided tour, step through the locked gate, and stand on the ground where 1,200 people once suffered, in the shadow of the tomb of the man who put them there.

Whether the Mackenzie Poltergeist is an angry spirit, a residue of mass suffering, or the most powerful case of collective suggestion ever recorded, Greyfriars remains singular: a beautiful old graveyard with a locked corner that has, by the hundreds, sent people away hurt. Few haunted places come with a warning quite so literal.

Frequently Asked

What is the Mackenzie Poltergeist?
A reported entity at Greyfriars Kirkyard, linked to the tomb of Sir George "Bloody" Mackenzie, who persecuted the Covenanters. Since 1999, hundreds of visitors near the Covenanters' Prison have reported being scratched, bruised, struck, or made to collapse, making it one of the best-documented poltergeist cases in the world.
Who was Bloody Mackenzie?
A 17th-century Scottish lawyer and Lord Advocate who brutally persecuted the Covenanters, many of whom died imprisoned. He earned the nickname "Bloody Mackenzie" and was buried in a mausoleum at Greyfriars near the prison where they suffered. His tomb is the focus of the haunting.
What is the Covenanters' Prison?
A section of Greyfriars where around 1,200 Covenanter prisoners were held outdoors in brutal conditions in 1679, and many died. It is kept locked and accessible only on guided tours, and is the most active part of the kirkyard, where most reported attacks occur.
Can you visit Greyfriars Kirkyard?
Yes. The kirkyard in Edinburgh is open and free to enter. Coordinates: 55.9469 N, 3.1911 W. The main graveyard is open, but the Covenanters' Prison is locked and accessible only via official guided ghost tours, especially popular at night and around Halloween.
